A Spreadsheet Way of Knowledge 🖊️
Steven Levy • Harper's Magazine (Republished on Medium) • November 1984
Technology reporter Steven Levy writing, in 1984, about a mysterious new piece of software taking over America: the spreadsheet. One of the most striking aspects of the piece is its capture of that era’s reaction to what was then a new technological and computing paradigm. “Models”, “cursors”, and “inputs” are such novel concepts that they have to be put in quotation marks and explained. Those who know how to wield the new technology are portrayed as having unlocked formidable, and potentially dangerous, hidden powers: one executive might use spreadsheets to identify a takeover target, another to conclude that the company’s pension plan ought to be scuttled. Levy dwells at some length on the idea that models are just models, as good as their assumptions and inputs, rather than privileged windows into reality. It’s all so redolently dated that it’s hard to believe this was just 40 years ago. The piece also touches on how spreadsheets upended the plodding “data processing” units within American corporations, and created new markets, like the one for spreadsheet software itself, worth $250 million in 1984.

Loitering towards war 🖊️
Anton Cebalo • Aeon • July 16, 2026
A vivid sketch of life in Vienna's coffeehouses and Belgrade's kafanas at the turn of the twentieth century. Secure in the apparent stability of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, Vienna's poets and artists have their gaze fixed firmly inward, mining their subjective experiences for creative and intellectual inspiration. Meanwhile, in nearby Belgrade, aggrieved Serbian nationalists gather to share war stories and foment revolutionary fervor, with the Empire's possession of Bosnia a particular focus of their ire. Among them was an impoverished Bosnian Serb student named Gavrilo Princip.

The Rise And Fall Of Liberal Democracy’s Shared Fiction 🖊️
Macario Schettino • Noema • July 30, 2026
An argument that new communication technologies unsettle the transcendent frameworks which human beings rely on for collective life, before facilitating the emergence of new and more stable alternatives. Newspapers in eighteenth-century Europe help bring about violent revolutions to overthrow ancient monarchies, before becoming the foundation of more stable citizen-centered political orders in the nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, radio and film are first used for wartime propaganda and the totalitarian demagoguery that leads to the Second World War, but then become the channels through which consensual national narratives are articulated. This pieces argues that, with the rapid spread of social media in the early twenty-first century, we are in the midst of another moment of rupture.
