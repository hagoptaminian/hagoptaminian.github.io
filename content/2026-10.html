Love with nowhere to go 🖊️
Derek van Zoonen • Aeon • October 27, 2026
A deeply moving reflection on grief. Although most prominently associated with the death of a loved one, the piece suggests that we experience grief in other circumstances when we lose something we love or care about greatly; we grieve, it says, because we love: grief is love with nowhere to go. Losses that cause grief are not mundane setbacks, but ones that foreclose future possibilities that we saw as central to our sense of self and identity. The person who loses a partner, the man who loses a vocation, the woman who finds out that she cannot conceive: such losses are often profoundly painful because they shut off imagined futures that were once deeply held. Although we are not accustomed to understanding such experiences as grief, the piece argues that this is exactly how they should be seen.
The AIs Are Not Going Rogue 🖊️
Ken Archer and Nobel Suhendra • Noema • October 1, 2026
Why recent “rogue AI“ incidents reveal not that AI models are becoming more generally intelligent, but a limit to their generality: their inability to critically reflect on their objectives and adjust course. Relentless “plot extenders,” AI models are extremely effective at using prior context to generate viable trajectories, but not at stepping outside of those plots and interrogating them. Rather than implying that the models are not dangerous, this framing situates the risk in the broader systems in which they are deployed.
The Sheer Size of It All 🖊️
Bill McKibben • New York Review of Books • November 5, 2026
A review of the work of Canadian photographer Edward Burtynsky. Burtynsky’s photographs, some dating back to the 1980s, seek to capture the sheer scale of the modern, industrial, capitalist world: urban mines in Ontario, assembly lines in China, ship-breaking yards in Bangladesh. Though often vertiginous and bleak, the resulting photographs are compelling to look at. Burtynsky, quoted in the piece, suggests that “one can [see them] as making political statements about the environment, but they also celebrate the achievements of engineering or the wonders of geology.”
The Slave's Power 🖊️
Drew Gilpin Faust • New York Review of Books • October 8, 2026
Historian Drew Faust reviews two recent titles on the history of American slavery. The piece grapples with recent scholarly debates over whether an accurate history of slavery is possible given the preponderance of archival sources created and maintained by the oppressors in that system, and the relative lack of sources from the victims themselves. Faust demonstrates how the authors she reviews use archival sources in inventive ways to bridge this gap. What emerges is history which both acknowledges its incompleteness but yields new discoveries and perspectives. Among the interesting findings the piece highlights is how enslaved teamsters (primarily men) and domestic servants (primarily women), by virtue of the travel involved in their work, encountered situations and ideas which enabled them to challenge slavery and prevailing social and racial hierarchies.
The Afterlife of a Fascist Murder: Giacomo Matteotti in Myth and Memory, 1924-2024 🎙️
Amy King • New Books Network • October 5, 2026
On the immediate and longer-term reactions to Giacomo Matteotti’s murder by Italian fascists in June 1924. Generating severe domestic and international backlash, the murder creates a crisis for Mussolini’s regime, which responds by clamping down on displays of mourning for Matteotti. The regime’s mouthpiece, Il Popolo d’Italia, suggests that Matteotti’s wife ought to grieve her loss with “silent dignity,” like the “countless fascist women who bear their mourning [in every province of Italy].” When Mussolini’s regime falls in 1943, Matteotti's memory bursts back into public space, helping support a narrative of enduring Italian anti-fascist resistance. In subsequent decades, right-wing Italian parties and governments repurpose his murder as a lesson in the importance of free speech.
One Hand Clapping: Unraveling the Mystery of the Human Mind 🎥
Nikolay Kukushkin • Harvard Science Book Talks and Research Lectures • March 7, 2026
Neuroscientist Nikolay Kukushkin speaking about his book One Hand Clapping. True to his self-description as a “molecular philosopher,” his ideas range from the atomic to the cosmic and everything in between. I found his thoughts on dopamine especially illuminating, and this interview with him also excellent.
The Commander: Fawzi al-Qawuqji and the Fight for Arab Independence 1914-1948 📕
Laila Parsons
On the storied life of Fawzi al-Qawuqji. Born in Tripoli, Lebanon, in 1890, as a young man he becomes one of the few Arab officers in the Ottoman Army, serving until the Empire's defeat in 1918. A resolute believer in a unified “Greater Syria,” over the next thirty years he becomes involved in one anti-colonial military confrontation in the region after another: he joins the anti-French Syrian rebellion in the middle of the 1920s, leads a unit in the 1936 Arab revolt in Palestine, fights the British in Iraq in the early 1940s, and then serves as field commander of the Arab Liberation Army in the 1948 Arab-Israeli War. In between, he spends several years in Jeddah as a military adviser to the embryonic Saudi state, and six years in Germany as a guest of the Nazi regime and then a prisoner of the occupying Soviet forces. After the defeat of the Arab armies in 1948, he retreats from military and public life, and dies in Beirut in 1977. The book is biography at its best, capturing not just the story of one man but the history of the region in the first half of the twentieth century.
